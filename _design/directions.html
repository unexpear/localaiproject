Why for this app

A control layer earns its name by being legible at a glance — every lane, every execution, every byte pulled. Bridge treats the screen as a printed broadsheet: rules between regions, monospaced counters for numbers, oxblood for things you should approve, amber for things downloading. No icons that don't carry data.

High density rewards attention; can feel "clinical" if you wanted a Jarvis to chat with. Closest to a Bloomberg terminal among the three.

Why for this app

The opposite premise to Bridge: instead of showing you everything, it shows you only the conversation, and the controls fade in only when you reach for them. Quiet color (paper white & sumi ink) with a single terra accent for moments that actually matter — pending approvals, retries, errors.

Calmest of the three, most "trustworthy"; needs more clicks to reach dense data (a Models view exists but it isn't the main act).

Why for this app

Where Bridge is editorial and Atelier is contemplative, Foundry is a tool you hold. Dark graphite chassis, panels separated by single-pixel seams, one orange LED in the status row that's actually wired to something (active install, pending approval). No filler chrome. The Cancel button looks like a real button on a Braun radio.

Most "Jarvis-feeling" of the three; familiarity tradeoff — if you've seen any modern dev tool you've seen the silhouette. Wins on implementability in QSS.

I'd lean C · Foundry as the safest implementable starting point — QSS handles the hairlines and the dark chassis perfectly, qt-material can host the LED accent, and it carries the "serious tool" personality you asked for. A · Bridge is the most distinctive, but it's the hardest to keep readable when the transcript fills with long messages. B · Atelier is gorgeous on the chat surface but will fight you when you go to the Models view — there's just a lot to show there.
